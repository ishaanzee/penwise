How to stop Adobe Acrobat opening PDFs in Chrome

If every PDF you click in Chrome now opens in Acrobat, with sign-in and AI prompts, the Adobe Acrobat extension is doing it. You can switch off just that behaviour, turn the extension off, or remove it, and get Chrome's own viewer back. Here's each step, checked against Adobe's and Google's help pages.

Updated October 2026 · 6 min read

First, to be fair to Adobe: lots of people like the extension. Its Chrome Web Store listing shows 349 million users and 4.38 stars from 64,263 ratings (as of October 2026). But the low-star reviews keep making the same points. One from October 2026 says it should "NOT come up automatically", and another from the same month asks Adobe to stop the AI pop-ups and sign-in prompts. If that's you, read on.

Step 1: turn off just the "open in Acrobat" behaviour

This keeps the extension installed but stops it taking over PDFs. Adobe's help mentions an Open all PDFs in Acrobat setting in the extension, and its Gmail and Google Drive page (updated August 2026) shows where the options are:

  1. In Chrome, open the three-dot menu, then Extensions → Manage extensions (or type chrome://extensions in the address bar).
  2. Find Adobe Acrobat: PDF edit, convert, sign tools and select Details.
  3. Select Extension options.
  4. Turn off the default view options for online links, Gmail and Google Drive. Adobe says these control "whether PDFs from Gmail, Google Drive, and online links open automatically in Acrobat."
  5. Select Save preferences.

For Gmail only, there's a shortcut: open a PDF from Gmail in the Acrobat viewer, select More options, and turn off Open PDFs from Gmail in Acrobat. The exact labels can change between extension updates, so look for the closest match.

Downloaded PDFs opening in the Acrobat app is a separate setting. Adobe says turning off the extension "does not stop PDFs from automatically opening in the Acrobat app after you download them in Chrome." To stop that on Windows, open Acrobat or Reader, press Ctrl + K, go to General and untick Always open PDFs downloaded from the web. In Chrome, you can also check your recent downloads, right-click a PDF and untick Always open files of this type (Chrome help).

Step 2: the AI Assistant and pop-ups

This is the hardest one to switch off on its own. As of October 2026, here's what Adobe documents:

  • For organizations: Adobe's enterprise configuration page (updated September 2026) describes a DisableGenAI policy. On Windows, an admin creates a string value named DisableGenAI, set to true, under HKLM\Software\Policies\Google\Chrome\3rdparty\extensions\efaidnbmnnnibpcajpcglclefindmkaj\policy, then checks it on chrome://policy. On macOS, Adobe gives an equivalent managed-preferences profile. You'll need admin rights, and it's meant for IT teams.
  • For the desktop app: Adobe's help page says to go to Preferences → Generative AI and untick Enable generative AI features. That applies to Acrobat and Reader on your computer, not the Chrome extension.

We couldn't find an Adobe help page that describes a per-user AI switch inside the Chrome extension. Check its Extension options page in case your version has one. If it doesn't, Step 1 keeps the extension out of the way until you click it, and Steps 3 to 5 remove it completely.

Step 3: turn off or remove the extension

From Chrome's help:

  • Turn it off (reversible): go to chrome://extensions and switch off the toggle on the Adobe Acrobat card. You can turn it back on any time.
  • Remove it: right-click the Acrobat icon in the toolbar and choose Remove from Chrome. Or, on chrome://extensions, select Remove on the Acrobat card and confirm.

Removing the extension doesn't uninstall Acrobat or Reader from your computer. It only affects Chrome.

Step 4: stop it coming back

Some reviewers say the extension "installed itself" (September 2026). Adobe's own deployment documentation explains where it often comes from. Its installer properties list says: "By default, installing the Continuous track of Reader installs the Chrome extension." Adobe's extension help page also says that once Reader is installed, "you'll receive the new viewer option the next time you open a PDF in Chrome."

  • If you don't use Acrobat Reader on your computer, uninstalling it removes the usual way the extension gets added back.
  • If you keep Reader, remove the extension again if it reappears after an install, and don't click Set as default when the Acrobat viewer offers it.
  • IT admins deploying Reader on Windows can set the installer property ENABLE_CHROMEEXT=0, which Adobe says "prevents it's install" (Adobe's wording).

We couldn't find Adobe documentation saying exactly when Reader updates re-add the extension on personal computers, so we haven't given steps for that.

Step 5: set Chrome's own PDF viewer back

  1. Type chrome://settings/content/pdfDocuments in the address bar. Or go to Settings → Privacy and security → Site settings → Additional content settings → PDF documents.
  2. Select Open PDFs in Chrome. (The other option, Download PDFs, saves every PDF instead of showing it.)

Open any PDF link to check. It should now open in Chrome's own viewer (Chrome help).

What to use instead

Chrome's built-in viewer (free, already there)

For reading, and for a lot of basic marking up, you may not need anything else. According to Google's help page, the viewer can highlight, draw, add typed text, fill forms and draw a signature. It also runs OCR on scanned PDFs on your device. It can't merge files or truly redact, and it can't save a signature to reuse yet, though Google is working on that. Our guide to Chrome's built-in PDF editor covers what it can and can't do.

Acrobat Reader on the desktop

If you liked Adobe's tools but not having them in the browser, the free Reader app still has Fill & Sign. Turn off the auto-open setting from Step 1's tip so it only opens when you choose.
